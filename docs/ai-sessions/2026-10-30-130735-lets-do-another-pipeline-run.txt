  (from $168,000 to $196,000), but the number of valid sales nearly tripled that
  year too, so part of the jump could come from which sales were included.

  The standard fix is a repeat-sales comparison: compare the same parcel's price
  at two sales, which removes the mix problem. That's the method behind the
  Case-Shiller index. There are 12,206 parcels with two or more valid sales,
  giving 13,530 sale pairs. That's enough for a county-wide yearly series and
  for larger areas.

  3. "Areas" needs a sensible unit. There are 175 municipalities, but 94 of them
  have fewer than 300 valid sales in 15 years, too few for year-by-year
  figures. Two options:
  - School districts (46): a median of about 1,600 valid sales each, enough for
    trends.
  - Municipalities with a minimum-sales threshold: smaller places would be
    grouped or left out.

  As we found earlier, these are the parcel's current municipality and school
  district, which is worth one line in the caveats. The data also has no
  property-type column, so we can't separate houses from commercial property.

  How I'd frame it:
  - Overall: How have Allegheny County sale prices changed since 2012? Show the
    yearly median and the repeat-sales index, side by side.
  - Years: Which years saw the biggest increases or decreases? Use
    year-over-year change in the repeat-sales index, with the median for
    comparison.
  - Areas: Where did prices rise fastest? Compare cumulative and annual
    appreciation by school district, and possibly by large municipalities.
  - Scope: valid sales only, in nominal dollars. Adjusting for inflation would
    need outside CPI data. That's optional, but worth considering, since part of
    the doubling is inflation.

  What it means for the gold layer: this settles the parcel/sales split. Repeat
  sales are built per parcel, and location comes from the parcel table. Gold
  would then have:
  - parcel
  - sale (market sales only)
  - sale_pair (repeat sales)
  - a yearly summary by area
